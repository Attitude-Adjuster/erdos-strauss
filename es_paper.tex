\begin{abstract}
For a prime $p$, put $A=(p+r)/4$, $r\equiv3\pmod4$, and $q=pA$. The
Erd\H{o}s--Straus equation has a solution at this $A$ exactly when a divisor of $q^2$
lies in the class $-q\pmod r$. We call the ordered search through
$r=3,7,11,\ldots$ the \emph{rung ladder}: for each $p$, we test successive rungs until
the first one gives a solution. The preceding rungs have failed for that $p$, even
though its ladder ultimately succeeds. We classify each such failure as an obstruction
by the Jacobi symbol, by another real character, by the multiplicative support
subgroup, or as a residual miss not explained by local support.

Among the $932{,}642{,}160{,}749$ hard-class primes below $10^{15}$, there are
$557{,}964{,}077{,}974$ unsuccessful rung tests: for each prime, we count and classify
every rung before its first successful one. Jacobi certificates explain
$99.1773\%$ and residual misses account for $0.8227\%$. Every support-only failure
occurs at $r=51$. The identity $4A=p+r$ explains this concentration: it forces $4$
into every support subgroup, a condition invisible to real characters. The maximum
first-hit rung is $155$. A proposed fit $M(X)=C\log X$ has the correct scale but does
not reproduce the long record plateaus.

At a fixed squarefree rung, Selberg--Delange theory gives an explicit
$c_r/\sqrt{\log X}$ law for the unrestricted factor-support model, while
$\chi_r((p+r)/4)=\chi_r(p)$ shows why the actual ladder is not independent. These
results separate a tractable local model from the cross-rung dependence still missing
from a proof.

An independent certified sieve verifies every prime below
$1.8446744065\times10^{19}$. Across $460$ shards,
$156{,}377{,}459{,}709$ primes reached the individual search, all were solved, and no
survivor remained. This is about $18.4$ times the previous record of $10^{18}$, but not
the full 64-bit range: the final $8{,}589{,}936{,}640$ integers below $2^{64}$ lie
outside the computation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The Erd\H{o}s--Straus conjecture \cite{Erdos1950} asserts that for every integer
$n \ge 2$ the equation $4/n = 1/x + 1/y + 1/z$ has a solution in positive integers; it
suffices to treat prime $n = p$. Polynomial congruence identities resolve the equation
outside a thin set of residue classes. Mordell's identities leave six classes
$p \equiv 1^2, 11^2, 13^2, 17^2, 19^2, 23^2 \pmod{840}$, all of them squares modulo
$840$ \cite{Mordell1969}. We denote their union by the \emph{hard class}
$\mathcal{H}$. The commonly used surrogate $p\equiv1\pmod{24}$ is four times larger and mixes
these primes with classes already settled by an identity, diluting the measured
residual share. For that reason, all counts below are restricted to $\mathcal{H}$.
The obstruction is structural: Schinzel and Yamamoto showed
that no finite list of polynomial congruence identities covers a quadratic-residue
class \cite{Schinzel2000,Yamamoto1965}.

Fixing $A$ and setting $r = 4A - p$, $q = pA$, solvability is equivalent to the existence of a
divisor of $q^2$ in the class $-q \bmod r$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:divisor}, classical).
A rung can fail for two quite different reasons:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Local residue support.} The divisors of $q^2$ all lie in some proper
subset of $(\mathbb{Z}/r)^\times$ determined by the residues of the primes of $q$, and
the target class lies outside it. A short certificate proves that the rung cannot work.
\item \emph{A residual miss.} The target class passes every local test, but none of the
finitely many divisors of $q^2$ reaches it.
\end{itemize}

The first case can be decided entirely by arithmetic in a finite group. The second is
the part left unexplained by local information. We formalize this distinction as a
nested hierarchy of certificates, use it to classify every failed rung for
$p<10^{15}$ in $\mathcal{H}$, and release certificates that can be checked
independently. The parametrization, character expansion, and Type I/II taxonomy are
classical. Our contribution is the hierarchy, a structural theorem restricting its
finest local level to an explicit finite list of rungs (\S\ref{sec:whyS}), the
exhaustive classification, a fixed-rung hazard calculation which retains the exact
dependence between $p$ and $(p+r)/4$, and the resulting picture of the slowly growing
residual tail.

The paper follows a single arc. Section~\ref{sec:known} assembles what is already
known --- Mordell's identities and the covering family --- into one statement: every
classical formula prescribes a rung and a witness divisor in advance.
Section~\ref{sec:ladder} dissolves that finite list into a geometric family, the
hyperbola of solutions at a fixed rung, and Section~\ref{sec:fourier} turns the
hyperbola's divisor lattice into a finite Fourier expansion whose vanishing modes are
exactly the certificate hierarchy. Section~\ref{sec:data} measures the hierarchy
exhaustively below $10^{15}$. Section~\ref{sec:record} then runs the arc in reverse:
the same classical formulas, algorithmically implemented in a certified covering sieve, verify
the conjecture to $1.8446744065\times10^{19}$. The final sections describe the
computation, place it against prior work, and state the analytic obstacle that remains.

\section{What is known, as one family}\label{sec:known}

The classical results on the conjecture are usually presented as a list of separate
identities. Read in the right coordinates they are one construction, and stating it
once sets up everything that follows.

Mordell's identities \cite{Mordell1969} are polynomial solutions of $4/p$ valid on
residue classes of $p$; together they settle every class modulo $840$ except the six
square classes defining $\mathcal H$ in the introduction. Behind them sits the covering family
of Rosati \cite{Rosati1954}, catalogued at scale by Salez \cite{Salez2014}: for
positive integers $a, c, d, k$ with $ck > a$, put $m = 4acd - 1$ and $e = ck - a$;
then every integer $p = mk - 4a^2d$ satisfies $p + k = 4ade$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:boxed}
\frac{4}{p} \;=\; \frac{1}{ade} + \frac{1}{acdp} + \frac{1}{cdep}.
\end{equation}
Nothing about primality is used: one triple $(a,c,d)$ solves the \emph{entire
arithmetic progression} $p \equiv -4a^2d \pmod m$ above an explicit floor. The family
is self-certifying --- the two-line verification of \eqref{eq:boxed} is the
certificate for every member at once.

The unifying reading is this. Writing $A = ade$, the identity gives $4A = p + k$, so
each formula fixes \emph{in advance} both the shifted value $A$ (equivalently, the
rung $k$ of the ladder introduced in \S\ref{sec:ladder}) and a divisor witnessing the
solution. A classical identity is a single precomputed point on one of the hyperbolas
of \S\ref{sec:ladder}. Two consequences shape this paper. Constructively, the
formulas can be implemented algorithmically: enumerated, certified, and swept over ranges, which
is how \S\ref{sec:record} verifies the conjecture to $1.8446744065\times10^{19}$.
Structurally, they cannot finish the job: no finite list of polynomial congruence
identities covers a quadratic-residue class \cite{Schinzel2000,Yamamoto1965}, and
\S\ref{sec:sweep} exhibits the obstruction concretely --- the family
\eqref{eq:boxed} can never produce a perfect square. On the six square classes
modulo $840$, solvability must therefore be established prime by prime, and the
question becomes not \emph{whether} a formula applies but \emph{how far down the
ladder} each prime must climb. This paper focuses on that ladder.

\section{The rung ladder and the divisor lattice}\label{sec:ladder}

Fix a prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and an integer $A > p/4$, and set
$r = 4A - p > 0$, $q = pA$. Subtracting $1/A$ from $4/p$ gives $1/B + 1/C = r/q$, and
completing the product turns this into a hyperbola:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hyperbola}
\frac{4}{p} = \frac{1}{A} + \frac{1}{B} + \frac{1}{C}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
(rB - q)(rC - q) \;=\; q^2 .
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[divisor criterion; classical]\label{lem:divisor}
Positive integer solutions $(B, C)$ of \eqref{eq:hyperbola} correspond bijectively to
divisors $u \mid q^2$ with $u \equiv -q$ and $q^2/u \equiv -q \pmod r$, via
$B = (u+q)/r$, $C = (q^2/u + q)/r$. If $\gcd(q, r) = 1$ the second congruence follows
from the first.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $(B,C)$ solves \eqref{eq:hyperbola} then $u = rB - q$ divides $q^2$ and is
$\equiv -q \pmod r$, as is its cofactor; conversely such a $u$ yields integers
$B, C \ge 1$. When $\gcd(q,r)=1$, $u \equiv -q$ gives
$q^2/u \equiv q^2(-q)^{-1} \equiv -q \pmod r$.
\end{proof}

As $A$ runs over integers exceeding $p/4$, $r$ runs over $r \equiv -p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.
We call the resulting search order a \emph{ladder} with \emph{rungs}
$r = 3, 7, 11, \dots$ and $A = (p+r)/4$; rung $r$ \emph{hits} if
Lemma~\ref{lem:divisor} produces a solution, and the \emph{depth} of $p$ is the
smallest hitting $r$.

For every rung considered in the computation, $q$ and $r$ are coprime.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:coprime}
If $0 < r < 3p$ then $\gcd(q, r) = 1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$r < 3p$ implies $A < p$, so $p \nmid A$ and, $p$ being prime,
$\gcd(A,p) = 1$. Then $\gcd(A, r) = \gcd(A, 4A - p) = \gcd(A, p) = 1$ and
$\gcd(p, r) = \gcd(p, 4A - p) = \gcd(p, 4A) = 1$ since $p$ is odd and $p \nmid A$.
Hence $\gcd(pA, r) = 1$.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:coprime} shows that the second congruence in
Lemma~\ref{lem:divisor} adds no further condition in our computation. Since $r/q$ is
reduced, the criterion also has a classical
two-unit-fraction form and a centered group-theoretic form.

\begin{proposition}[Rav's criterion in three forms]\label{prop:rav}
Let $\gcd(q,r)=1$ and write $q=\prod_\ell \ell^{e_\ell}$. The following are
equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Some $u\mid q^2$ satisfies $u\equiv-q\pmod r$.
\item Some coprime pair of divisors $d_1,d_2\mid q$ satisfies
$d_1+d_2\equiv0\pmod r$.
\item The fixed class $-1$ belongs to the centered exponent box
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exponent-box}
B_{q,r}:=\left\{\prod_\ell \ell^{f_\ell}\bmod r:
f_\ell\in\mathbb Z,\ |f_\ell|\le e_\ell\right\}
\subseteq(\mathbb Z/r)^\times .
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We pass between the three formulations as follows. Given the pair in (2), put
$t=(d_1+d_2)/r$, $B=qt/d_1$ and $C=qt/d_2$. These are integers and
$1/B+1/C=r/q$. Moreover
$u=rB-q=q\,d_2/d_1$ is a divisor of $q^2$, its complementary divisor is
$q\,d_1/d_2$, and $u\equiv-q\pmod r$.

Conversely, given $u$ in (1), set $g=\gcd(u,q)$, $d_1=q/g$ and $d_2=u/g$.
If $\ell^e\parallel q$ and $\ell^j\parallel u$, with $0\le j\le2e$, then
\[
v_\ell(d_1)=\max(0,e-j),\qquad
v_\ell(d_2)=\max(0,j-e).
\]
Thus $d_1$ and $d_2$ are coprime divisors of $q$. Because $g$ is invertible
modulo $r$, we can cancel it from $gd_2=u\equiv-q=-gd_1\pmod r$ and obtain
$r\mid d_1+d_2$. The two constructions are inverse; swapping $d_1,d_2$
corresponds to replacing $u$ by $q^2/u$.

Finally, $u/q=d_2/d_1$ has exponent vector
$f_\ell=v_\ell(d_2)-v_\ell(d_1)\in[-e_\ell,e_\ell]$. As $u$ ranges over
the divisors of $q^2$, these are exactly the vectors in \eqref{eq:exponent-box},
and $u\equiv-q\pmod r$ becomes $u/q\equiv-1\pmod r$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[attribution and role]\label{rem:rav}
Form (2) is Rav's classical criterion for a reduced fraction $m/n$ to be a sum
of two unit fractions, here with $m/n=r/q$ \cite{Rav1966}; sometimes this criterion is
attributed to Schinzel, and was communicated to us in this form, by Elsholtz \cite{ElsholtzPC2026}.
Chen and Koo state the criterion in this form \cite{ChenKoo2013}. Form (1) is the completed hyperbola
\eqref{eq:hyperbola}. Form (3) is the group-theoretic reparametrization used
below: the support subgroup $H$ of Definition~\ref{def:hierarchy} is generated
by the same residues after the exponent bounds are removed. Levels J, RC and S
say that the subgroup already misses $-1$; level R says that the subgroup
contains $-1$ but the finite box does not.
\end{remark}

The divisors of $q^2 = p^2A^2$ form a box lattice: writing $A = p^{e}A'$ with
$p \nmid A'$ and $A' = \prod_i \ell_i^{a_i}$, they are $u = p^{\,j}d$ with
$0 \le j \le 2e+2$ and $d \mid A'^2$. Reducing modulo $r$ colors this grid, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:divisor} asks whether the color $-q$ appears. The classical two-type
taxonomy is the row coordinate:

\begin{proposition}[types are rows]\label{prop:types}
Suppose $p \nmid A$ and $\gcd(q,r) = 1$, and let a solution arise from
$u = p^{\,j}d$ with $j \in \{0,1,2\}$ and $d \mid A^2$. If $j = 1$ then $p \mid B$ and
$p \mid C$ \textup{(}Type II\textup{)}; if $j \in \{0,2\}$ then $p$ divides exactly one
of $B, C$ \textup{(}Type I\textup{)}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$p \mid q$, so $p \mid u + q$ iff $p \mid u$, i.e.\ iff $j \ge 1$; the cofactor has
$p$-exponent $2-j$. Since $\gcd(r,p)=1$, $p \mid B$ iff $p \mid u+q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
For $p = 73$, $A = 20$ ($r = 7$, $q = 1460$) the lattice contains four solution
divisors $u \le q$: $u = 10, 80$ in row $j = 0$ (Type I, e.g.\
$4/73 = 1/20 + 1/210 + 1/30660$) and $u = 73, 584$ in row $j = 1$ (Type II, e.g.\
$4/73 = 1/20 + 1/219 + 1/4380$). Both classical families sit in one lattice.
\end{remark}

\section{The character expansion and a hierarchy of certificates}\label{sec:fourier}

Throughout, $\gcd(q,r) = 1$ and
$N_r(p) = \#\{u \mid q^2 : u \equiv -q \ (\mathrm{mod}\ r)\}$, counting all divisors
(each unordered solution $\{B,C\}$ contributes two).

\begin{theorem}[divisor count in a class; orthogonality]\label{thm:fourier}
With $q^2 = \prod_\ell \ell^{\,a_\ell}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fourier}
N_r(p) \;=\; \frac{1}{\varphi(r)} \sum_{\chi \ (\mathrm{mod}\ r)}
\overline{\chi(-q)} \prod_{\ell \mid q}
\bigl( 1 + \chi(\ell) + \chi(\ell)^2 + \cdots + \chi(\ell)^{a_\ell} \bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $u, a$ coprime to $r$, orthogonality \cite[Ch.~4]{Davenport2000} gives
$\varphi(r)^{-1}\sum_\chi \chi(u)\overline{\chi(a)} = \mathbf{1}[u \equiv a]$. Take
$a = -q$, sum over $u \mid q^2$, and factor the completely multiplicative $\chi$ across
the divisor structure of $q^2$.
\end{proof}

The conjugate in \eqref{eq:fourier} is essential: omitting it counts the class
$(-q)^{-1}$ rather than $-q$. Both evaluations were checked against direct
enumeration; the examples are included in the repository tests.

The trivial character contributes $\tau(q^2)/\varphi(r) > 0$; the others contribute
cancellation. For real characters that cancellation is itself a divisor count:

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:interference}
Let $\chi$ be a real character mod $r$. Since every exponent $a_\ell$ in $q^2$ is even,
\[
\sum_{u \mid q^2} \chi(u) \;=\; \prod_{\chi(\ell) = 1}(a_\ell + 1) \;=\; \tau\bigl((q^2)_+\bigr) \;\ge\; 0,
\]
where $(q^2)_+$ is the part of $q^2$ supported on primes with $\chi(\ell) = 1$: at
primes with $\chi(\ell) = -1$ the local factor $1 - 1 + \cdots + 1$ equals $1$.
\end{lemma}

The obstruction applies to every odd $r$ and every real character.

\begin{corollary}[real-character certificate]\label{cor:blocking}
Let $r$ be odd with $\gcd(q,r) = 1$ and let $\chi$ be any real Dirichlet character
mod $r$. If $\chi(\ell) = +1$ for every prime $\ell \mid q$ and $\chi(-q) = -1$, then
$N_r(p) = 0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$\chi$ is completely multiplicative, so every divisor $u \mid q^2$ has $\chi(u) = +1$,
whereas $\chi$ is constant on residue classes mod $r$ and equals $-1$ at $-q$. Hence no
divisor lies in that class. Equivalently in \eqref{eq:fourier}: by
Lemma~\ref{lem:interference} the $\chi$ term is $\chi(-q)\tau(q^2) = -\tau(q^2)$,
cancelling the trivial term exactly.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[the Jacobi symbol is only one of them]\label{rem:whichchi}
For odd $r = \prod_{i=1}^{\omega} p_i^{e_i}$ the group $(\mathbb{Z}/r)^\times$ has
exactly $2^{\omega}$ real characters, namely the products
$\chi_T(x) = \prod_{i \in T} \jac{x}{p_i}$ over subsets
$T \subseteq \{1,\dots,\omega\}$. The Jacobi symbol is one of them---but \emph{not}
in general the full-subset one: $\jac{\cdot}{r} = \chi_{T_0}$ with
$T_0 = \{i : e_i \text{ odd}\}$, since $\jac{x}{p_i^{e_i}} = \jac{x}{p_i}^{e_i}$. For
squarefree $r$ these coincide; for $r = 63 = 3^2\cdot 7$ the Jacobi symbol is
$\jac{\cdot}{7}$ and for $r = 75 = 3\cdot 5^2$ it is $\jac{\cdot}{3}$, and both of
these rungs occur in our data. For prime $r$ there is a single nontrivial real
character and the distinction is vacuous; for composite $r$, testing only the Jacobi
symbol is strictly weaker than testing all real characters. Note also that
$\jac{x}{r} = +1$ does not mean $x$ is a quadratic residue when $r$ is composite, so
the hypothesis must be phrased in terms of the character, not of residuosity.
\end{remark}

The criteria simplify considerably once one uses $r \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, and the
simplification explains exactly which rungs can exhibit which levels.

\begin{proposition}[criteria in reduced form]\label{prop:reduced}
Let rung $r$ fail for $p$, and let $H \le (\mathbb{Z}/r)^\times$ be as in
Definition~\ref{def:hierarchy}. Recall $r \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A real character $\chi$ mod $r$ certifies the failure if and only if
$\chi(-1) = -1$ \textup{(}$\chi$ is odd\textup{)} and $H \le \ker\chi$. In
particular the Jacobi symbol certifies if and only if $\jac{\ell}{r} = +1$ for every
prime $\ell \mid q$. Once this holds, $\jac{-q}{r}=-1$ follows from
$r\equiv3\pmod4$.
\item $-q \in H$ if and only if $-1 \in H$. Hence the support certificate of
Definition~\ref{def:hierarchy} says exactly that $-1 \notin H$.
\item There are exactly $2^{\omega(r)-1}$ odd real characters mod $r$, one of which is
the Jacobi symbol. Consequently a level-\textup{RC} failure can occur only when
$\omega(r) \ge 2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $q$ is a product of the primes generating $H$, we have $q \in H$; so if
$H \le \ker\chi$ then $\chi(q) = 1$ and $\chi(-q) = \chi(-1)$, giving (1). For the
Jacobi symbol, $\jac{-1}{r} = (-1)^{(r-1)/2} = -1$ because $r \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.
Part (2) is the same observation: $-q \in H \iff -1 \in H$ because $q \in H$. For (3),
$\chi_T(-1) = \prod_{i \in T}(-1)^{(p_i-1)/2}$, so $\chi_T$ is odd exactly when $T$
meets $\{i : p_i \equiv 3 \ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)\}$ in an odd number of elements; that
set is nonempty since $r \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, and the number of such $T$ is
$2^{\omega-1}$. When $\omega = 1$ there is therefore exactly one odd real character,
which must be the Jacobi symbol, leaving no room for level \textup{RC}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[certificate hierarchy]\label{def:hierarchy}
Let rung $r$ fail for $p$ (so $\gcd(q,r) = 1$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:coprime}), and let
$H \le (\mathbb{Z}/r)^\times$ be the subgroup generated by
$\{\ell \bmod r : \ell \mid q\}$. The failure has level
\begin{description}
\item[J] if the Jacobi symbol $\jac{\cdot}{r}$ satisfies Corollary~\ref{cor:blocking};
\item[RC] if some real character mod $r$ does, but the Jacobi symbol does not;
\item[S] if no real character does, but $-q \bmod r \notin H$;
\item[R] (\emph{residual miss}) otherwise: $-q \in H$, yet the divisors of $q^2$---whose
exponents are bounded---miss the class.
\end{description}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:nested}
The levels are nested. Every J-certificate is an RC-certificate, and every
RC-certificate implies $-q \notin H$. Level R is what remains after all local support
information is exhausted.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first claim is Remark~\ref{rem:whichchi}. For the second, if $\chi$ is trivial on
every $\ell \mid q$ then $H \le \ker\chi$, while $\chi(-q) = -1$ puts $-q$ outside
$\ker\chi$.
\end{proof}

Levels RC and S are finer tests in principle. In particular, S detects obstructions
from higher-order characters and from subgroups of index greater than $2$ that no real
character sees. Section~\ref{sec:data} measures how often these finer tests are needed
and where they first appear. Section~\ref{sec:whyS} also shows that level S is confined
to an explicit finite set of rungs.

For the smallest rung, level R is empty unconditionally, because modulo $3$ there are
only two characters.

\begin{proposition}[rung 3 has no residual misses]\label{prop:rung3}
Let $p \equiv 1 \pmod{12}$ and $A = (p+3)/4$, so $r = 3$. Then rung $3$ hits unless
its failure is detected by a real character. More explicitly, it fails precisely when $3 \nmid A$
and every prime $\ell \mid pA$ satisfies $\ell \equiv 1 \pmod 3$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $3 \mid A$ take $u = 3$: then $3 \mid q$, $9 \mid q^2$, and both congruences of
Lemma~\ref{lem:divisor} read $0 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$, so rung $3$ hits. If $3 \nmid A$
then $\gcd(q,3) = 1$ and \eqref{eq:fourier} has two terms,
$N_3(p) = \tfrac12\bigl(\tau(q^2) + \chi(-q)\,\tau((q^2)_+)\bigr)$ with $\chi$ the
nontrivial character mod $3$ (real, so conjugation is immaterial). If some $\ell \mid q$
has $\chi(\ell) = -1$ then $\tau((q^2)_+) < \tau(q^2)$ and $N_3 > 0$ either way. If
every $\ell \mid q$ has $\chi(\ell) = 1$ then $\tau((q^2)_+) = \tau(q^2)$ and
$\chi(-q) = \chi(-1) = -1$, so $N_3 = 0$; since $\jac{\cdot}{3}$ is the only nontrivial
real character mod $3$, this is a level-J certificate.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Which rungs can carry a level-S failure}\label{sec:whyS}

No real character detects a level-S failure. In the computation, every such failure
occurs at the single rung $r=51$ (\S\ref{sec:data}). Higher-order characters still
detect the support obstruction in the centered Fourier formulation. The concentration at $r=51$ is explained by a
constraint on $H$ that every real character misses.

\begin{lemma}[$4$ is already in the finite box]\label{lem:four}
For every rung under consideration, $4\in B_{q,r}$ and hence $4\in H$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$4A=p+r$ gives $pA^{-1}\equiv4\pmod r$. Because $\gcd(q,r)=1$, the prime $p$
does not divide $A$, so $p$ and $A$ are coprime divisors of $q=pA$. Their ratio
$p/A$ is one of the points of
$B_{q,r}$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:rav}. Thus $4\in B_{q,r}\subseteq H$.
\end{proof}

The same identity has a character-valued form which is useful for predicting how
often the first certificate level occurs.

\begin{corollary}[exact coset identity]\label{cor:coset}
For every real character $\chi$ modulo $r$,
\[
\chi(A)=\chi(p).
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The congruence $4A\equiv p\pmod r$ gives
$\chi(A)=\chi(p)\chi(4)^{-1}$, while
$\chi(4)=\chi(2)^2=1$.
\end{proof}

Any real $\chi$ has $\chi(4) = \chi(2)^2 = 1$, so every such character has
$4\in\ker\chi$. The condition cannot be detected by the character test in
Corollary~\ref{cor:blocking}. It does,
however, impose a strong restriction on the support subgroup.

\begin{proposition}[when level S is possible]\label{prop:Spossible}
Write $G = (\mathbb{Z}/r)^\times$. Rung $r$ can carry a level-S failure only if some
subgroup $H \le G$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sadm}
4 \in H, \qquad -1 \notin H, \qquad (-1)H \in (G/H)^2 ,
\end{equation}
and any $H$ satisfying \eqref{eq:Sadm} does produce one whenever it is realized as a
support subgroup. In particular level S is impossible at every prime rung.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first condition is Lemma~\ref{lem:four}, the second is
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduced}(2), and the second also forces the rung to fail, since all
divisors of $q^2$ lie in $H$. For the third: the real characters trivial on $H$ are
exactly the characters of $G/H$ of order dividing $2$, and by
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduced}(1) the failure escapes every real-character certificate
precisely when all of them take the value $+1$ at $-1$. The intersection of the kernels of
all order-$\le 2$ characters of a finite abelian group is its subgroup of squares, which
is the stated condition.

If $r$ is prime then $G$ is cyclic of order $r - 1 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$. Then $-1 \notin H$
forces $|H|$ odd, so $G/H$ is cyclic of order $2m$ with $m$ odd and $(-1)H$ is its unique
element of order $2$ --- not a square, since $4 \nmid |G/H|$. No $H$ qualifies.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:Srungs}
Among $r \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ with $r < 300$, level S is possible only for
\[
r \;\in\; \{51,\ 119,\ 123,\ 187,\ 195,\ 219,\ 255,\ 267,\ 287,\ 291\},
\]
and $51$ is the smallest by a wide margin. At $r = 51$, $119$ and $123$ the admissible
subgroup is unique and cyclic of index $4$; at $r = 51$ it is
$H = \langle 2 \rangle = \{1,2,4,8,13,16,26,32\}$ inside
$(\mathbb{Z}/51)^\times \cong \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/16$.
\end{corollary}

Dropping Lemma~\ref{lem:four} from \eqref{eq:Sadm} would admit fourteen rungs below $140$
alone,
\[
15,\ 35,\ 39,\ 51,\ 55,\ 75,\ 87,\ 91,\ 95,\ 111,\ 115,\ 119,\ 123,\ 135,
\]
with nothing to distinguish $51$, and with $r = 15$ visited far more often than
$r = 51$. The condition $4\in H$ eliminates eleven of the fourteen. This also shows
that the concentration at $r=51$ is not simply an artifact of which rungs are reached:
level-RC failures occur at $r = 51, 75, 91, 99, 115$. Thus these rungs are present in
the data and can fail for local reasons. Nevertheless, at $75$, $91$ and $115$ every
subgroup that would otherwise satisfy \eqref{eq:Sadm}
omits $4$, and at $99$ none exists in the first place.

This gives a simple check on any future computation:
\textbf{a level-S failure can never occur at
$r = 15, 35, 39, 55, 75, 87, 91, 95, 111, 115$ or $135$}. The next rung at which one
is possible is $r=119$.

\subsection{A numerical check of logarithmic record growth}\label{sec:logdepth}

Elsholtz asked whether the record might be modeled by $C\log X$
\cite{ElsholtzPC2026}. Table~\ref{tab:depth} gives ten cumulative observations,
$X=10^6,\ldots,10^{15}$. If the proposed $N$ denotes the number of tested primes
rather than the interval endpoint, then $\log N=\log X-\log\log X+O(1)$ by the prime
number theorem; these ten record values cannot distinguish the two scales. We use the
endpoint $X$, which is the quantity recorded by the census. A least-squares fit
constrained to pass through the origin gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logfit}
M(X)\approx 4.531\log X
       =10.433\log_{10}X,
\end{equation}
with a root-mean-square error of $15.53$ rungs. Allowing an intercept gives
\[
M(X)\approx38.13+7.055\log_{10}X,
\]
with root-mean-square error $11.83$ and $R^2=0.746$. The constrained fit happens to
predict $156.5$ at $10^{15}$, close to the observed $155$, but this endpoint agreement
is retrospective and should not be confused with a successful prediction.

As a simple forward check, fitting only through $10^{13}$ gives
$M(X)=4.664\log X$ in the constrained model. It predicts $150.3$ and $161.1$ at
$10^{14}$ and $10^{15}$, compared with $131$ and $155$. The affine fit on the same
training range predicts $132.6$ and $138.8$: it follows the next plateau but misses the
jump to $155$. Thus a logarithm gives a reasonable scale for the record, but one
constant does not describe the plateaus and jumps visible in the data.

There is also a statistical limitation. These are cumulative maxima, so the ten points
are strongly dependent; a record is repeated until a rarer prime appears. A genuine
extreme-value model must start from the probability that a prime survives each rung and
must retain the dependence between successive values $(p+r)/4$. The fixed-rung
calculation below supplies one part of such a hazard model, but not yet the cross-rung
joint law. We therefore regard $C\log X$ as a plausible descriptive scale, not an
estimated growth law.

All failures at $r=3$ are level J, as Proposition~\ref{prop:rung3} predicts. Residual
misses first appear at $r=11$ and are concentrated at small rungs, while the finer local
levels occur only on the thin subsequence of composite rungs. Their full counts and
localization are reported below on the same population as the main census.

\subsection{What the statistics say}\label{sec:statistics}

The main empirical facts can be stated briefly. From $10^6$ to $10^{15}$, the
cumulative residual share falls monotonically from $3.2634\%$ to $0.8227\%$, although
the residual count grows to $4{,}590{,}145{,}833$. Residual misses are concentrated at
divisor-poor shifted values, but a random-occupancy model for the reachable residue
classes fails by orders of magnitude. The divisors of $q^2$ are too dependent for
their count alone to predict a hit.

At a fixed squarefree rung, the unrestricted factor-support calculation is more
successful. Selberg--Delange theory gives
$N_r(Y)\sim c_rY/\sqrt{\log Y}$ with an explicit Euler-product constant. The exact
identity
$\chi_r((p+r)/4)=\chi_r(p)$ and the primality of $p=4A-r$ supply corrections absent
from an independent-integer model. After matching both effects, hard-class primes in a
sample below $1.2\times10^7$ still have level-J rates $1.33$--$3.82$ times those of the
affine-prime control. This is a finite-range effect, not yet an asymptotic constant.

These calculations explain why the logarithmic fit in \S\ref{sec:logdepth} is only a
scale model. It would require a joint survival law across successive rungs, whereas the
available analysis gives marginal hazards at six early rungs.

\subsection{Which primes are actually hard}\label{sec:hardclass}

Mordell's identities \cite{Mordell1969} settle $4/n$ for every $n$ except possibly those
$n$ that are \emph{squares} modulo $840$. There are exactly six such classes,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hardclass}
\mathcal{H} \;=\; \{1,\ 121,\ 169,\ 289,\ 361,\ 529\} \pmod{840},
\end{equation}
being $1^2, 11^2, 13^2, 17^2, 19^2, 23^2$. All six lie in $1\pmod{24}$, but that coarse
class contains $24$ reduced classes modulo $840$, only six of which are squares. The
other three quarters have already been settled, yet their early failed rungs would
still enter the denominator of any reported failure share.

\begin{observation}[the easy primes contribute only level J]\label{obs:dilution}
Through $10^{11}$, the censuses of $(1\bmod24)$ and $\mathcal{H}$ have identical depth
counts at every rung $r\ge11$ and identical level-RC, level-S, and level-R totals. Every
extra failure from $(1\bmod24)\setminus\mathcal{H}$ occurs at $r=3$ and has a Jacobi
certificate. Restricting to $\mathcal{H}$ therefore removes only already-explained
failures and preserves the entire residual and deep-ladder population in the overlap.
\end{observation}

Including the coarse classes thus understates the residual share. All subsequent depth
and failure-share statistics use $\mathcal{H}$.

\subsection{The main empirical pattern}\label{sec:thesis}

The data show one clear pattern, but it is important not to confuse that pattern with
two stronger claims that the computation cannot establish.

\begin{quote}
\emph{As the cutoff increases, local certificates account for a growing share of the
failed rungs. The remaining residual misses are rare and concentrated at divisor-poor
shifted values.}
\end{quote}

Formally we separate three hypotheses, which are often conflated and have very
different status.

\begin{description}
\item[H1 (local dominance).] Among failed rungs \emph{of primes in $\mathcal{H}$}, the
residual share tends to zero:
\[
\Pr\bigl(\text{level R} \,\big|\, \text{failure},\ p \le X\bigr) \longrightarrow 0
\qquad (X \to \infty).
\]
This is the hypothesis tested directly by the data. \emph{It does not imply the
Erd\H{o}s--Straus conjecture}: a vanishing share is compatible with a growing number of
residual misses and says nothing about whether one prime can miss every rung.
\item[H2 (divisor threshold).] For each rung $r$ there is a threshold $f(r)$ with
$\tau(q^2) \ge f(r) \Rightarrow N_r(p) > 0$ whenever the target class is reachable.
Residual misses still occur at $\tau(q^2)=81$, so no small constant threshold is
visible. A threshold depending on $r$ remains open, and Section~\ref{sec:occupancy}
shows why naive occupancy cannot establish one.
\item[H3 (unbounded but slow depth).] There is no uniform constant $R$ with
$D(p)\le R$ for every $p$, but the record envelope
\[
M(X)=\max_{p<X,\ p\in\mathcal H}D(p)
\]
grows exceptionally slowly. The move from $107$ through $131$ to $155$ after long
plateaus is
consistent with this hypothesis and inconsistent with treating the old record as a
natural barrier. Finite computation cannot distinguish genuine unboundedness from a
larger constant bound, so H3 is deliberately stated as the numerical expectation, not
as a conclusion.
\end{description}

\subsection{A fixed-rung hazard calculation}\label{sec:rung-hazard}

The logarithmic record fit above needs a probability that a prime survives one more
rung. The simplest part can be calculated: the factor-support component of a level-J
obstruction at a fixed rung. Let $r$ be squarefree and let
$\chi_r=\jac{\cdot}{r}$. If $A$ were an unrestricted integer, the requirement that
every prime divisor of $A$ lie in $\ker\chi_r$ is a classical
Selberg--Delange problem. Writing
\[
N_r(Y)=\#\{A\le Y:\chi_r(\ell)=+1\text{ for every prime }\ell\mid A\},
\]
one obtains from Selberg--Delange theory \cite[Ch.~II.5]{Tenenbaum2015}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sd-hazard}
N_r(Y)\sim c_r\frac{Y}{\sqrt{\log Y}},\qquad
c_r=\frac{L(1,\chi_r)^{1/2}H_r(1)}{\sqrt\pi},
\end{equation}
where
\[
H_r(1)^2=
\prod_{\ell\mid r}\left(1-\frac1\ell\right)
\prod_{\substack{\ell\nmid r\\ \chi_r(\ell)=-1}}
\left(1-\frac1{\ell^2}\right).
\]
Indeed, the relevant Dirichlet series factors as
$F_r(s)=(\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_r))^{1/2}H_r(s)$. The square-root singularity at
$s=1$ gives the power $(\log Y)^{-1/2}$ and the constant in
\eqref{eq:sd-hazard}. Direct enumeration at $r=3$ and independent class-number
evaluations of the required $L$-values check the constants for
$r=3,7,11,15,19,23$.

The unrestricted model is not the census model. Corollary~\ref{cor:coset} forces
$A$ and $p$ into the same real-character class modulo $r$. Conditioning on that class
introduces the first-order factor $2r/\varphi(r)$. Primality introduces a second local
bias: for a prime $\ell\nmid2r$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:affine-prime-bias}
\Pr\left(\ell\mid A\mid p=4A-r\text{ prime}\right)
\approx\frac1{\ell-1},
\end{equation}
not $1/\ell$, because one of the $\ell$ residue classes would make $p$ divisible by
$\ell$ and is removed. Thus neither the residue of $A$ nor its prime support can be
sampled as that of an independent random integer.

The effect is visible numerically in Table~\ref{tab:hazard-control}.

\subsection{Against random-divisor models: an occupancy test}\label{sec:occupancy}

$e^{-m}$ with $m = \tau(q^2)/|H|$, so a large lattice would force a hit. We tested this
directly on the recorded $p\equiv1\pmod{24}$ rung sample below $2 \times 10^8$, retaining
only rungs whose target class is reachable
(levels HIT and R), binning by $m$ and comparing the observed miss rate to $e^{-m}$
evaluated at the \emph{lower} edge of each bin---the most generous value the null takes
anywhere in the bin.

relevant configurations are precisely the atypical ones. Any proof along this route
must account for their multiplicative structure.

\subsection{How not to model the ladder}\label{sec:model}

The rungs of one prime are not independent trials. They share $p$, while the shifted
values $(p+3)/4,(p+7)/4,\ldots$ are consecutive integers with correlated
factorizations. A statistical model must therefore treat the ladder as a
rung-dependent survival process, not as repeated Bernoulli sampling. The observed
shares are descriptive marginals; confidence intervals based on independence would be
misleading.

Residual misses are nevertheless consistently divisor-poor: their median
$\tau(q^2)$ is $27$, compared with $81$ at hitting rungs in the matched summaries. This
is a correlation, not a consequence of \eqref{eq:fourier}; a small main term makes a
zero possible, not probable.

This leaves two parts of the covering problem:

\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{local layer}, entirely finite: which rungs carry a real-character or
support certificate is a computation in $(\mathbb{Z}/r)^\times$ given the residues of
the primes of $q$;
\item a \emph{residual layer}: prove that level-R misses cannot occupy every rung of
some prime. This is the content the conjecture actually needs, and H1 does not supply
it---a vanishing share of failures is not a statement about any individual ladder.
\S\ref{sec:occupancy} shows the natural probabilistic route is blocked, and
\S\ref{sec:doorways} that the natural analytic ones are too.
\end{itemize}

\section{Verification to \texorpdfstring{$1.8446744065\times10^{19}$}{1.8446744065e19}}\label{sec:record}

The census asks how each hard-class prime fails before it succeeds. The complementary
question --- is every prime in a range solvable at all? --- is answered far faster by
running the arc of \S\ref{sec:known} in reverse: instead of following each prime down
its ladder, apply the classical formulas algorithmically until only a vanishing residue of
primes needs individual attention. The strategy --- sweep the covering family's
parameters rather than search, extend the wheel far beyond the six classes modulo
$840$, and finish the thin residue with a direct solver that factors $(p+r)/4$ ---
was outlined to us by Elsholtz \cite{ElsholtzPC2026}. The two computations are
orthogonal by design; in the covering identity $p + k = 4ade$ the parameter $k$ is a
rung of size roughly $p/m$, some nine orders of magnitude deeper than the census ever
searches, and covers are never applied to the census population, whose statistics
they would contaminate.

\subsection{Two certificate families and a derived wheel}\label{sec:wheel}

Two families of class-level certificates drive the sieve. The first (F1) is the
covering family \eqref{eq:boxed} itself: reparametrized by $N = acd$ and
$\alpha = c^2d$ it is exactly $\{(N,\alpha) : \alpha \mid N^2\}$ with modulus
$m = 4N - 1$, and this completeness has a structural blind spot --- the modulus is
always $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$, so F1 can never constrain a residue modulo $8$. The second
family (F2) closes the gap: \emph{uniform rung certificates}, pairs $(r, u)$ with
$\gcd(r,u)=1$ for which both divisor conditions of Lemma~\ref{lem:divisor} are forced
by the class of $p$ modulo $L = 4\,\mathrm{lcm}(r,u)$ alone, so that a single
exact-arithmetic check certifies the whole class. Together the two families
\emph{derive} the classical reduction: applied to the classes modulo $840$ they
reproduce exactly Mordell's six square classes, so Mordell's theorem is demoted from
an input to a regression check.

At the production modulus $M = 2{,}042{,}040$ the derivation kills $366{,}332$ of the
$368{,}640$ unit classes --- each with a named certificate recorded in a published
table --- leaving $2{,}308$ surviving lanes, about one touched position per $885$
integers. Nothing is transcribed from the literature (Terzi's published class table
is reported to contain small errors \cite{ElsholtzPC2026}); the table is regenerated
at startup by every scanner,
which refuses to run on any digest mismatch, and re-derived independently by a
dependency-free exact-arithmetic verifier.

\subsection{The pipeline and its discipline}\label{sec:pipeline}

A window of integers passes through five stages. First, the wheel keeps only the
$2{,}308$ difficult residue classes. Second, $3{,}745$ additional filters certify
arithmetic progressions which do not fit into the wheel modulus. Third, deterministic
Miller--Rabin separates primes from composites. The surviving primes then enter the
rung walk of Lemma~\ref{lem:divisor}, which emits one witness divisor per prime. A
direct search through \eqref{eq:boxed} is retained as a final fallback. If both
per-prime searches fail, the number is written as a \emph{survivor} and the run fails.

The bulk stages certify $99.995101\%$ of the wheel positions. Miller--Rabin leaves
$156.377$ billion primes; every one is solved by the rung walk, the direct
fallback is never needed, and the survivor count is zero. The $6.26$\,TB deterministic
certificate stream is committed shard by shard by SHA-256. Selected streams were
reproduced byte for byte, and more than nine million certificates were checked in
exact arithmetic, including an exhaustive window at the upper edge. The full audit is
published with the run metadata rather than repeated here.

\subsection{The 64-bit boundary, and the record}\label{sec:boundary}

The natural ceiling of the implementation is not a power of ten. The direct solver
probes $n = p + 4a^2d \le p + 2048$ and factors it by methods whose integer square
root is exact below $(2^{32}-1)^2$; both scanners therefore refuse to start above
\[
P_{\max} \;=\; (2^{32}-1)^2 - 2049 \;=\; 18{,}446{,}744{,}065{,}119{,}614{,}976,
\]
which is $8{,}589{,}936{,}640$ below $2^{64}$. Every modular product in the fast paths
is checked against the frozen reference on adversarial inputs around $2^{63}$. The
scanners refuse an interval crossing $P_{\max}$ rather than allowing an arithmetic
limit to appear as a mathematical survivor. The verification used $460$ shards in two
runs, split at $10^{19}$.

The conclusion is: \emph{every prime $255{,}255\le p<P_{\max}$ admits an
Erd\H{o}s--Straus decomposition}. The lower range is already covered by the censuses.
Thus the conjecture is verified to about $1.8446744065\times10^{19}$, extending the
previous record of $10^{18}$ \cite{MihneaBogdan2025} by a factor of about $18.4$. The
honest statement stops there. It is not a verification of the final
$8{,}589{,}936{,}640$ integers below $2^{64}$ and hence not of the full 64-bit range.
The published class tables, filter tables, shard summaries, stream digests, and
exact-arithmetic verifier allow any part of the computation to be regenerated without
repeating the entire run.

\section{The computation}\label{sec:computation}

To classify all failed rungs, we must completely factor every shifted value
$A=(p+r)/4$ encountered before the first hit. Factoring these values independently is
unnecessarily expensive; their arithmetic-progression structure permits a bulk sieve.

\subsection{Bulk sieve-factorization}\label{sec:bulk}

Within one of the six classes modulo $840$, write $p=p_0+840j$. At a fixed rung,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:apA}
A=\frac{p+r}{4}=A_0+210j, \qquad A_0=\frac{p_0+r}{4}.
\end{equation}
For every prime $\ell\nmid210$,
\[
\ell\mid A \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
j\equiv-A_0\,\overline{210}\pmod\ell.
\]
For each $\ell$, the sieve updates exactly the entries divisible by $\ell$. Sieving through
$\sqrt{A_{\max}}$ leaves a prime cofactor, eliminating primality tests and Pollard rho
from the main computation. Once only a thin set of primes remains on the ladder, the scanner
switches back to per-value factorization. Against the naive implementation this gives
speedups of $7.3\times$, $12.8\times$, and $20.3\times$ at $10^{10}$, $10^{11}$, and
$10^{12}$, respectively, with identical output.

\subsection{Verification protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

The optimized scanners (CPU and GPU) are checked against an independent
exact-arithmetic reference and against the earlier per-value implementation. Repeated
runs, thread counts, window sizes, and independent shardings give identical counts.
The final shards ($192$ at $10^{13}$, $400$ at $10^{15}$) are checked for gaps and
overlaps, and every fine-certificate and deep-prime record---$20{,}001$ at $10^{13}$,
$585{,}677$ at $10^{15}$---is re-derived in exact arithmetic on a machine different
from the one that produced it. The verifier additionally enforces
Proposition~\ref{prop:Spossible} mechanically. If a claimed level-S failure occurs at
an inadmissible rung, or if its support subgroup omits $4$, the verifier reports a
contradiction to the proposition rather than treating the record as a simple format
error. Each reported solution is verified by the integer identity
$4ABC=p(AB+BC+CA)$.

\subsection{Identity sweeps and the quadratic obstruction}\label{sec:sweep}

The census and a record-height verification solve different computational problems
(\S\ref{sec:record}): verification needs each integer covered once, by any identity;
classification needs every failed rung preceding the first hit. The identity-first
approach uses the classical Type~I family of \S\ref{sec:known} (Mordell
\cite{Mordell1969}; used as a large-scale presieve by Salez \cite{Salez2014}): every
positive value
\begin{equation}\label{eq:typeI-family}
n=(4acd-1)k-4a^2d,\qquad a,c,d,k\in\mathbb Z_{>0},
\end{equation}
admits an Erd\H{o}s--Straus decomposition. A finite parameter sweep is therefore an
efficient way to cover many integers. However, this family can never produce a square.

\begin{proposition}[no square occurs in the Type I family]\label{prop:nosquare}
For positive integers $a,c,d,k$, the integer in \eqref{eq:typeI-family} is not a
perfect square.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $M=4acd-1$ and suppose that $n=Mk-4a^2d=s^2$. Since
$4acd\equiv1\pmod M$,
\[
n(2cd)^2\equiv-4a^2d\,4c^2d^2=-(4acd)^2d\equiv-d\pmod M.
\]
Thus $-d$ would be a square modulo $M$. But $\gcd(d,M)=1$ and
$\jac{-d}{M}=-1$. Indeed, $M\equiv3\pmod4$ gives $\jac{-1}{M}=-1$.
For every odd prime $\ell\mid d$, quadratic reciprocity and
$M\equiv-1\pmod\ell$ give
\[
\jac{\ell}{M}=(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\jac{M}{\ell}
=(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\jac{-1}{\ell}=1;
\]
if $2\mid d$, then $M\equiv7\pmod8$ and $\jac{2}{M}=1$. Multiplication over
the prime factors of $d$ yields $\jac{d}{M}=1$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

For the main Type~I family, this proposition is an elementary instance of the
quadratic obstruction behind the theorems of Schinzel and Yamamoto
\cite{Schinzel2000,Yamamoto1965}: no finite collection of polynomial congruence identities
covers an entire quadratic-residue class. Prime inputs are not squares, so this does
not obstruct any one prime; it explains why a fixed finite identity sweep cannot
replace the rung-by-rung census on $\mathcal H$.

Elsholtz also noted $288$, $336$, and approximately $9545$ as small composite
exceptions in an unrestricted parameter sweep \cite{ElsholtzPC2026}. We do not need an exhaustive claim
about that composite exception set: the production sieve is a prime verification and
starts at $255{,}255$, while the lower range is handled independently. The relevant
part of the suggestion is the change of enumeration order---generate the progressions
from bounded $(a,c,d)$ and apply them in bulk, then use a direct solver only on what
remains---which is exactly the pipeline of \S\ref{sec:record}.

\section{Relation to prior work}\label{sec:prior}

The divisor-congruence criterion of Lemma~\ref{lem:divisor} is classical and appears in
several equivalent guises. Rav's coprime-divisor criterion \cite{Rav1966}, as presented
for example by Chen and Koo \cite{ChenKoo2013}, is equivalent to it by
Proposition~\ref{prop:rav}; the centered exponent box is the same criterion after the
change of variables $u/q=d_2/d_1$. Mordell's identities \cite{Mordell1969} and the modular
equations catalogued by Salez \cite{Salez2014} are, in the present language, individual
rungs: a polynomial identity valid on a residue class of $p$ prescribes $A$ (and hence
$r$) together with a divisor $u$ whose residue is guaranteed to be the target class.
Yamamoto \cite{Yamamoto1965} and Schinzel \cite{Schinzel2000} established the
quadratic obstruction that Corollary~\ref{cor:blocking} localizes to a single rung.
Elsholtz and Tao \cite{ElsholtzTao2013} give the Type I / Type II taxonomy used in
Proposition~\ref{prop:types}, together with mean-value theorems for the number of
solutions; their framework subsumes the parametrization here. Vaughan \cite{Vaughan1970}
bounded the exceptional set by $\ll N\exp(-c(\log N)^{2/3})$. Prior to this work the
conjecture was verified numerically to $10^{18}$ \cite{MihneaBogdan2025}, after Swett
\cite{Swett1999} and Salez \cite{Salez2014}; \S\ref{sec:record} extends the record to
$(2^{32}-1)^2 - 2049$. A recent preprint \cite{Unified2026} obtains density one
within $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ using divisor conditions rather than congruence identities,
and is the closest antecedent to the perspective taken here.

We therefore make no novelty claim for the divisor-lattice or coprime-pair criterion.
Their ingredients are known. What we add is the certificate hierarchy of
Definition~\ref{def:hierarchy}, its exhaustive measurement, and the released
certificates.

\section{What remains}\label{sec:remaining}

The local part is finite. At a fixed rung, the character expansion identifies every
subgroup obstruction, and the census measures what remains when those obstructions
vanish. The unresolved part is cross-rung: prove that no prime can miss the target
class in every finite exponent box
\[
B_{q_r,r},\qquad q_r=p(p+r)/4,\qquad r=3,7,11,\ldots .
\]
The difficulty is that the group and the factorization both change with $r$.
Selberg--Delange theory describes fixed character restrictions for typical integers,
but primality and the affine relation $4A=p+r$ couple the prime factors of successive
shifted values. Standard large-sieve or independent-hazard arguments do not retain
that dependence.

A useful next result would be either a product-set theorem forcing $-1\in B_{q,r}$ once
the subgroup obstructions disappear, or a cross-rung cancellation estimate showing
that the Fourier errors cannot all cancel their positive main terms. Even an
almost-all version would test whether the observed plateaus and jumps arise from a
slow extreme-value law. A uniform statement for every prime would prove the
conjecture; this is why the remaining step is not a modest extrapolation of the census.

The second concrete question concerns level S. The group calculation says exactly
which rungs can support it; below $10^{15}$ only $r=51$ is occupied, although $119$
and $123$ are admissible and reached. Predicting the first occurrence at a second rung
requires the missing distribution of support subgroups along $(p+r)/4$.

\section{Directions: Fourier interference and its obstacles}\label{sec:doorways}

The Fourier analogy becomes exact after centering the divisor lattice. Write
$q=\prod_\ell \ell^{e_\ell}$ and let $G_r=(\mathbb Z/r)^\times$. A divisor
$u=\prod_\ell\ell^{j_\ell}$ of $q^2$ determines, in $G_r$, the centered point
$u/q=\prod_\ell\ell^{k_\ell}$ with $k_\ell=j_\ell-e_\ell$ and
$-e_\ell\leq k_\ell\leq e_\ell$. Thus the moving target $-q$ becomes the fixed target
$-1$. Define the multiplicity measure
\[
 \nu_{q,r}(g)=\#\left\{(k_\ell): -e_\ell\leq k_\ell\leq e_\ell,
       \ \prod_{\ell\mid q}\ell^{k_\ell}=g\text{ in }G_r\right\}.
\]
Then $N_r(p)=\nu_{q,r}(-1)$. With the convention
$\widehat\nu(\chi)=\sum_{g\in G_r}\nu(g)\chi(g)$, and writing
$\chi(\ell)=e^{i\theta_{\chi,\ell}}$, its finite Fourier transform factors as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centered-transform}
 \widehat\nu_{q,r}(\chi)
 =\prod_{\ell^{e_\ell}\parallel q}D_{e_\ell}(\theta_{\chi,\ell}),
 \qquad
 D_e(\theta)=\sum_{k=-e}^{e}e^{ik\theta}
 =\frac{\sin((2e+1)\theta/2)}{\sin(\theta/2)},
\end{equation}
where the quotient has the limiting value $2e+1$ at $\theta=0$. Fourier inversion
therefore rewrites Theorem~\ref{thm:fourier} as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centered-inversion}
 N_r(p)=\frac{1}{\varphi(r)}\left\{
 \tau(q^2)+\sum_{\chi\neq\chi_0}\chi(-1)
 \prod_{\ell^{e_\ell}\parallel q}D_{e_\ell}(\theta_{\chi,\ell})\right\}.
\end{equation}
Every local factor is real, and $\chi(-1)=\pm1$. A miss is therefore literal
destructive interference: the nonprincipal spectrum cancels the positive mass
$\tau(q^2)$ exactly.

This gives a deterministic, if not yet uniform, non-cancellation certificate. Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interference-mass}
 \mathfrak I_r(p)=\sum_{\chi\neq\chi_0}
 \prod_{\ell^{e_\ell}\parallel q}
 \frac{|D_{e_\ell}(\theta_{\chi,\ell})|}{2e_\ell+1}.
\end{equation}
\begin{proposition}[spectral non-cancellation]\label{prop:spectral-noncancellation}
The triangle inequality in \eqref{eq:centered-inversion} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spectral-lower}
 N_r(p)\ \geq\ \frac{\tau(q^2)}{\varphi(r)}\bigl(1-\mathfrak I_r(p)\bigr),
 \qquad
 \mathfrak I_r(p)<1\ \Longrightarrow\ N_r(p)>0.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each normalized product in \eqref{eq:interference-mass} is the absolute value of the
corresponding nonprincipal term in \eqref{eq:centered-inversion}, divided by
$\tau(q^2)$. The inequality follows by subtracting their sum from the principal term.
\end{proof}
Unlike a random-occupancy model, \eqref{eq:spectral-lower} is exact and retains the
dependence among divisors. Its weakness is also precise: it discards the signs between
nonprincipal modes.

The hierarchy now has a spectral reading. Recall the centered box
\[
 B_{q,r}=\prod_{\ell^{e_\ell}\parallel q}
 \{\ell^{-e_\ell},\ldots,1,\ldots,\ell^{e_\ell}\}\subseteq G_r .
\]
Then $N_r(p)>0$ exactly when $-1\in B_{q,r}$. A J- or RC-certificate is a maximally
coherent odd real mode, trivial on every generator of the support subgroup $H$. At
level S the same obstruction is detected by an odd higher-order character in the
annihilator of $H$; it is invisible only if one restricts attention to real characters.
At level R, $-1\in H$ but the finite exponent box still misses it, so no annihilator
character separates the target: cancellation is spread among partially damped modes.
Equivalently, this is a product-set problem. Finite-group product-set estimates turn
sufficiently small expansion of the successive factors in $B_{q,r}$ into a stabilizer
subgroup, while expansion without a stabilizer is the combinatorial counterpart of
Fourier damping. This structure-versus-mixing dichotomy is the main line of evidence
behind the interference picture.

\paragraph{Direction I: spectral mixing on one rung.}
The immediate aim is to control \eqref{eq:interference-mass} from the orders of the
residues $\ell\bmod r$ and the exponents $e_\ell$. If a nonprincipal character is
nontrivial on several prime factors of $q$, the corresponding normalized Dirichlet
kernels are damped. One needs enough such damping collectively to make
$\mathfrak I_r(p)<1$, or a signed refinement of \eqref{eq:spectral-lower} when the
absolute-value bound is too weak. Product-set growth offers a dual route: prove
that $B_{q,r}$ either has a proper stabilizer, already recorded by the hierarchy, or
covers $-1$ once its exponent box is sufficiently rich. This formulation explains why
$\tau(q^2)$ alone cannot be a threshold: the phases and their multiplicities matter.

\paragraph{Direction II: decorrelation across the ladder.}
For $q_r=p(p+r)/4$, let $E_r(p)$ denote the nonprincipal part of
\eqref{eq:centered-inversion}, including the factor $1/\varphi(r)$, and set
$\mathcal R_p(R)=\{r\leq R:r\equiv3\pmod4,\ \gcd(q_r,r)=1\}$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ladder-interference}
 \sum_{r\in\mathcal R_p(R)}N_r(p)
 =\sum_{r\in\mathcal R_p(R)}
   \frac{\tau(q_r^2)}{\varphi(r)}
 +\sum_{r\in\mathcal R_p(R)}E_r(p).
\end{equation}
(A noncoprime rung already hits by Lemma~\ref{lem:coprime}.) Hence total interference
cannot persist through rung $R$ if
\[
 \left|\sum_{r\in\mathcal R_p(R)}E_r(p)\right|
 <\sum_{r\in\mathcal R_p(R)}\frac{\tau(q_r^2)}{\varphi(r)}.
\]
This is the rigorous form of the proposed ``no total interference'' mechanism. The
difficulty is that both the group and the signal change with $r$: the signal at rung
$r$ is built from the factorization of $q_r$, so a standard large-sieve estimate does
not apply unchanged. What is needed is a cross-rung uncertainty principle
for these arithmetically coupled spectra.

\paragraph{Direction III: arithmetic input for the moving signal.}
All analytic routes meet the same shifted-factorization obstacle. The Frobenius class
of $p$ controls $\chi(p)$, but not the prime factors of $(p+r)/4$. Selberg--Delange
theory gives useful models for the frequency with which all those factors lie in a
fixed subgroup---for an index-two condition, a count of order
$c_rx(\log x)^{-1/2}$ follows from the usual branch-point argument
\cite[Ch.~II.5]{Tenenbaum2015}---but conditioning on $p$ prime turns the question into a
multiplicative correlation along a linear form. Chebotarev alone does not see that
correlation, and sieve methods encounter the parity barrier.

For residual modes, the natural input is divisor distribution: Hooley's
$\Delta$-function \cite{Hooley1979}, Hall--Tenenbaum \cite{HallTenenbaum1988}, and
Ford's work on divisors in prescribed ranges \cite{Ford2008}. Those theories control
typical integers; here one needs uniformity for the structured sequence
$q_r^2=p^2((p+r)/4)^2$, ultimately for every $p$. No known method makes that jump. The
Fourier formulation does not remove this obstacle, but identifies the quantities that
must be controlled: local phase damping on one rung or signed spectral decorrelation
across several rungs.

\section{Problems}\label{sec:problems}

\begin{problem}[spectral census]\label{prob:sd}
Augment the census with the normalized local factors in
\eqref{eq:interference-mass}, the signed error $E_r(p)$, and the margin
$1-\mathfrak I_r(p)$. Determine how often \eqref{eq:spectral-lower} certifies a hit,
which characters dominate residual misses, and whether every computed ladder contains
a rung with $\mathfrak I_r(p)<1$. Section~\ref{sec:rung-hazard} completes the first
fixed-rung calculation for level J. Extend it to the hard-class local factors, to RC
and S modes, and most importantly to joint hazards across successive rungs. That joint
law is what a derivation of logarithmic record growth would require.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[structure versus mixing]\label{prob:divisors}
Prove a nontrivial condition on the orders of $\ell\bmod r$ and the exponents $e_\ell$
under which $-1\in H$ forces $\mathfrak I_r(p)<1$. Equivalently, give a product-set
theorem forcing $-1\in B_{q,r}$ once stabilizer obstructions have been excluded.
Even a result for prime $r$, or for squarefree $q$, would separate genuine subgroup
confinement from finite-box interference. A bound depending only on $\tau(q^2)$ cannot
capture this distinction.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[cross-rung non-cancellation]\label{prob:interference}
Find a range $R=R(p)<p$ on which the signed spectral errors in
\eqref{eq:ladder-interference} satisfy
\[
 \left|\sum_{r\in\mathcal R_p(R)}E_r(p)\right|
 <\sum_{r\in\mathcal R_p(R)}\frac{\tau(q_r^2)}{\varphi(r)}.
\]
Any such estimate for every $p\in\mathcal H$ proves that some rung hits. An average or
almost-all version would not prove the conjecture, but would test whether the observed
slow growth of record depth is explained by decorrelation rather than by a fixed bound.
Identify precisely what replacement for the large sieve can accommodate the moving
factorizations $q_r=p(p+r)/4$.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:bound}
Determine whether $M(X)=\max_{p<X,\ p\in\mathcal H}D(p)$ is unbounded. Short of that,
derive a tail estimate for $D(p)$ strong enough to distinguish a slowly diverging record
from a fixed ceiling, and compare record setters with the smallest spectral margin
$\min_{r<D(p)}|1-\mathfrak I_r(p)|$ on their failed rungs. A bound $D(p)=o(p)$ for
every sufficiently large $p$ would already prove the conjecture on $\mathcal H$ after
finite verification, so it is not a modest intermediate target.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:tail}
Corollary~\ref{cor:Srungs} says which rungs \emph{can} carry a level-S failure; it does
not say which do. Below $10^{15}$ only $r = 51$ is occupied, though $r = 119$ and $123$ are
admissible and are visited by every ladder deeper than them --- such ladders exist (the
record is $155$), and level RC now demonstrably reaches $123$. Estimate the density of primes whose support subgroup at rung $119$ or
$123$ equals the unique admissible subgroup there, and predict the height at which a second
occupied rung should first appear. A prediction that survives one further decade would turn
Corollary~\ref{cor:Srungs} from a constraint into a model.
\end{problem}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The certificate hierarchy explains almost every failed rung in the hard class, usually
through the Jacobi symbol alone. What remains is small in proportion but not in count:
the residual share falls to $0.8227\%$, while $4.59$ billion residual misses remain
below $10^{15}$. Those misses occur disproportionately at divisor-poor shifted values,
yet their divisors are far too dependent modulo $r$ for a random-occupancy argument. The
level that no real-character certificate detects is also highly concentrated. Because
$4A=p+r$ puts $4$ in every finite exponent box, and hence in every support subgroup,
level S can occur at only ten rungs below $300$. Every observed instance occurs at the
smallest of them.

The depth data carries a separate message. The maximum first-hit rung stayed at $107$
for a long interval and then rose through $131$ to $155$, with intermediate depths
populated. This is the pattern expected from a very thin extreme tail. It provides no
evidence for a uniform $O(1)$ depth bound. The suggested model $M(X)=C\log X$ gives
$C=4.531$ and the correct order of magnitude, but misses the observed record sequence
by $15.53$ rungs root-mean-square and does not reproduce its long plateaus. Our
numerical expectation is that the record depth is unbounded but grows extraordinarily
slowly; the range $p<10^{15}$ is not enough to identify that growth law.

The fixed-rung analysis makes the limitation more precise. In an unrestricted model,
the factor-support component of the level-J hazard has the explicit scale
$c_r/\sqrt{\log X}$. In the actual ladder,
$\chi_r((p+r)/4)=\chi_r(p)$ exactly, and the primality of $p$ further changes the local
factorization probabilities. Even after these effects are included in an affine-prime
control, hard-class primes have level-J rates larger by factors $1.33$--$3.82$ at the
six rungs tested. These are now measured departures from the independent model, not a
general warning that dependence may exist. What remains unknown is their behavior at
height and their dependence across rungs.

None of this proves the conjecture. A vanishing share of residual failures does not
prevent one prime from missing every rung, and an observed record is neither an upper
bound nor a theorem of divergence. The useful outcome is the separation itself: local
obstructions are finite and certifiable, while the remaining problem concerns the
structured divisors of $p^2((p+r)/4)^2$ along shifted prime values. That is the part a
proof must control.

The verification of \S\ref{sec:record} puts a computational floor under that
separation. Every prime below $(2^{32}-1)^2-2049$ is solvable; the small initial range
is covered by the censuses and the verification sieve covers the rest. Equivalently,
the combined computation reaches $1.8446744065\times10^{19}$ with zero survivors. The
class-level certificates are published directly, while the per-prime streams are
hashed and can be regenerated shard by shard. The final $8{,}589{,}936{,}640$ integers
below $2^{64}$ remain outside the claim. The two products therefore meet in a narrower
but useful statement: the verification shows solvability throughout the computed
range, while the classification says which part of that solvability is not explained
by local arithmetic.

\section*{Data and code availability}\label{sec:data-availability}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Data and code availability}

The complete repository is
\url{https://github.com/Attitude-Adjuster/erdos-strauss}. Questions, corrections, code
problems, and independent reproduction reports may be submitted at
\url{https://github.com/Attitude-Adjuster/erdos-strauss}.

The repository contains the exact-arithmetic reference implementation, the
independent C\raisebox{0.2ex}{\small ++} scanners (the frozen reference and an
optimized variant proved output-identical against it), the CUDA scanner, the
certificate verifier---including the mechanical check of
Proposition~\ref{prop:Spossible}---the per-shard summaries of both censuses ($192$
shards at $10^{13}$, $400$ at $10^{15}$), the merged depth and level histograms, the
$20{,}001$ fine-certificate and deep-prime records of the $10^{13}$ census, the
$585{,}677$ of the $10^{15}$ census, and the verification transcripts. The analysis
scripts reproduce the cumulative and disjoint-decade statistics. Level-J failures are
summarized rather than stored individually because each is regenerated directly from
$(p,A,r)$.

For the verification sieve of \S\ref{sec:record}, the repository additionally contains
the certified class tables at both wheel moduli (surviving classes plus a named
killing certificate for every other unit class, with digests), the pruned filter
tables, both sieve implementations (the frozen reference and the optimized scanner,
byte-identical on shared ranges), the independent exact-arithmetic verifier for the
covering identity and class tables, and all $460$ metadata records from the two
record-height runs. These records contain the interval, counters, provenance, and
SHA-256 commitment for each certificate stream. The repository also contains the
re-derived shard edges and samples, the complete certificate set for the smaller
verification window, and summaries and digests for the exhaustive window at the upper
edge of the computation.

Each solution certificate is a triple $(p,A,u)$ and is checked through
$4ABC=p(AB+BC+CA)$. The per-shard summaries are published so that small pieces of the
census can be reproduced independently rather than requiring the full run.

Direct enumeration also checks the conjugated character formula on the small examples
used during development; the full table is retained in the source and repository tests.
